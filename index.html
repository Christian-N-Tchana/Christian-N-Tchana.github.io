About me

My doctoral research investigates how agricultural practices, particularly pesticide use and organic farming, shape host–parasite interactions and zoonotic risks in farmland specialist birds. By combining field ecology, parasitology, ecotoxicology, and statistical modelling, my PhD research aims to better understand how pesticide exposure influences parasitic infections and potential zoonotic risks.

More broadly, I am interested in disease ecology, conservation biology, ecotoxicology, and how global environmental change affects wildlife health and pathogen transmission dynamics.

Current projects

Agricultural practices, parasitism and zoonotic risks in farmland birds (PhD project)

2025–2028 | Centre d’Études Biologiques de Chizé (CEBC–CNRS), La Rochelle University, France

My doctoral research, conducted at La Rochelle University and the Centre d’Études Biologiques de Chizé (CEBC–CNRS), examines how organic farming, conventional agricultural practices and pesticide exposure influence host–parasite interactions in birds associated with agricultural landscapes. The project is based in the Zone Atelier Plaine & Val de Sèvre (western France), a long-term study system where agricultural practices and landscape characteristics can be linked to ecological measurements.

Axis 1 — Parasite communities and zoonotic agents. I investigate the diversity, prevalence and, where relevant, infection intensity of several groups of organisms associated with birds: intestinal parasites such as Cryptosporidium, potentially pathogenic or opportunistic fungi, blood parasites and ectoparasites. This axis also assesses whether agricultural context is associated with variation in organisms of potential zoonotic concern, while distinguishing pathogen detection from demonstrated transmission risk.

Axis 2 — Consequences for avian hosts. The second axis explores how infection and pesticide exposure, separately or together, may be associated with bird body condition and selected phenotypic or physiological traits. Combining field data, parasitological analyses, environmental exposure metrics and ecological modelling should help identify relationships among agricultural practices, parasite infections and wildlife health.

PhD supervisors: Prof. Jérôme Moreau and Dr Karine Monceau (CEBC–CNRS / La Rochelle University).

Urban greenspaces, avian diversity and zoonotic risk

University of Yaoundé I, Yaoundé, Cameroon; research across urban green spaces

The project aims to document variation in bird species richness, community composition and ecological characteristics among green spaces exposed to different levels of urban disturbance. It also examines avian parasites and selected agents of potential zoonotic importance to explore patterns of infection across bird species and habitats. Field sampling combines bird surveys and capture-based sampling, with parasitological analyses and ecological comparisons planned or under way.

The research seeks to understand the biodiversity value of urban green spaces and the ecological factors associated with parasite infections, while distinguishing the detection of potentially zoonotic agents from demonstrated human transmission risk.

Collaborators and poster co-authors: Danielle Manewoung, Anaelle Bakami, Aures Yemdji, Litila Franc, Carmel Diffouo, Yollande Gueohada, Gabriella Ondoua and Sévilor Kekeunou.

Promoting conservation of agricultural birds (ProCAB project)

Littoral Region, Cameroon · University of Yaoundé I

This project explores how agricultural management affects farmland bird communities in the Littoral Region of Cameroon. Agricultural intensification, pesticide use and habitat simplification can alter the availability of food and nesting resources, while differences in shade-tree cover and vegetation structure may create contrasting conditions for birds across farms.

The project aims to describe farming practices and identify environmental pressures, assess bird richness and community composition across agricultural settings, and examine how habitat features and pesticide-management practices relate to bird assemblages. Field surveys and farmer interviews are intended to connect ecological observations with the local agricultural context. The research also supports capacity building through student participation and aims to inform feasible bird-friendly management and outreach.

Research collaborators: Daniella Manewoung, Crinan Jarrett and Anaelle Bakami.

Past projects

Host stress and transmission-stage investment in avian malaria (Master 2 project)

January–July 2024 | UMR MiVEGEC, Montpellier, France

For my second-year Master’s research project at UMR MiVEGEC, I investigated how host physiological stress may alter within-host dynamics and transmission-related traits of the avian malaria parasite Plasmodium relictum, SGS1 lineage. The study used domestic canaries (Serinus canaria) as the vertebrate host model and focused on the effects of elevated corticosterone, a glucocorticoid involved in the physiological stress response.

I examined several complementary components of parasite infection: total parasitaemia, gametocytaemia, the conversion of asexual parasites into gametocytes, and the relative production of male and female gametocytes. Considering these measures together makes it possible to distinguish changes in parasite abundance from changes in investment in transmissible stages. Particular attention was paid to gametocyte sex allocation because only gametocytes can continue the parasite life cycle following ingestion by an appropriate mosquito vector.

The project combined an experimental host–parasite approach with parasitological measurements and the analysis of stress-related physiological variation. It contributed to my interest in how environmental conditions experienced by hosts can influence parasite life-history strategies and opportunities for transmission.

Supervisors and research mentors: Ana Rivero, Claire Loiseau, Arnaud Berthomieu and Valentin Chauvin (UMR MiVEGEC). Collaboration: Frédéric Angelier (CEBC–CNRS).

Nest-dwelling ectoparasites and long-term plumage coloration in blue tits (Master 1 project)

March–September 2023 | UMR CEFE, Montpellier, France

During my first-year Master’s research project at the Centre d’Écologie Fonctionnelle et Évolutive (CEFE), I examined the consequences of nest-dwelling, blood-feeding Protocalliphora larvae for blue tits (Cyanistes caeruleus) in Corsica. These parasites develop in nests and feed on nestlings, potentially affecting early growth and the acquisition of traits later expressed in adult plumage.

The main objective was to investigate whether exposure during development leaves measurable carry-over effects on condition and coloration after fledging. The study also examined whether parasites encountered while breeding could influence adult parental coloration. We considered plumage traits with potential relevance for visual signalling, including yellow coloration and ultraviolet-blue crown coloration, and asked whether effects differed among offspring, mothers and fathers.

The research linked field-based nest monitoring, ectoparasite load assessments and quantitative colour measurements. Results highlighted moderate but persistent associations between parasite exposure and certain coloration traits, with effects depending on age, sex or breeding context rather than being uniform across individuals.

Supervisors: Claire Doutrelant, Claire Loiseau, Amélie Fargevieille and Lisa Sandmeyer (UMR CEFE, Montpellier).

Bird and bat diversity in Lake Ossa Wildlife Reserve, Cameroon (Master project)

August 2019–January 2020 | University of Yaoundé I, Cameroon

My Zoology Master’s research focused on bird and bat diversity in the Lake Ossa Wildlife Reserve, a wetland and forest-associated landscape in the Littoral Region of Cameroon. Birds and bats provide complementary perspectives on vertebrate biodiversity because they occupy partly overlapping habitats while differing in activity periods, mobility, foraging strategies and ecological roles.

The project aimed to inventory both taxonomic groups, describe their assemblages and examine their ecological use of habitats, including possible dimensions of niche overlap. Field surveys included mist-netting and complementary observations, enabling the documentation of species occurrence, community structure and variation among sampled sites. By examining birds and bats within the same protected landscape, the study sought to contribute baseline knowledge relevant to local biodiversity assessment and conservation planning.

The research also provided practical experience in tropical field ornithology, bat surveys and community-level biodiversity analysis. Such baseline surveys are particularly valuable in Central African ecosystems where biodiversity information remains incomplete and pressures on natural habitats are changing.

Supervisors: Prof. Sévilor Kekeunou and Prof. Eric Moïse Bakwo Fils. Equipment support: IDEA WILD.
